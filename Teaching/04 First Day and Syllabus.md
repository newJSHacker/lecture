# 04 — First day and syllabus

The first meeting teaches students how this course will feel. They decide, in the first 20 minutes, whether effort will be rewarded and whether confusion is allowed.

Do not spend the first day reading the syllabus aloud.

## Goals of day one

1. They can name the course goal in one sentence.
2. They have produced one small visible thing.
3. They know how to get help and how they will be graded.
4. You have seen who is already lost on install.
5. They have heard the integrity and AI rules in human language, not only in legal text.

## A 75-minute first-day script

| Minutes | Do this | Do not do this |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0–8 | Who you are, why this course exists, one picture of the end project | Your full CV |
| 8–18 | The contract: time, tools, grading, late work, AI | Reading 8 pages of policy |
| 18–28 | Names and a 60-second pair intro: “what I want to make” | Icebreakers unrelated to the work |
| 28–55 | A complete tiny win (see below) | Tooling lecture with no output |
| 55–70 | Clone the repo, run the starter, fix the one machine that fails | “Install this at home, good luck” |
| 70–75 | What to do before next time (one page, one task) | A 40-item reading list |

## Tiny wins that fit day one

Pick one. Finish it.

| Course | Tiny win |
| --- | --- |
| Intro programming | A page that prints their name and a random number |
| Web technologies | A heading, a color, inspect it in DevTools |
| Computer Graphics I | A cube they can rotate with the mouse |
| Computational geometry | Three points and a colored `orient` |
| WebGL | One triangle in a single HTML file |
| Three.js | A glTF they did not model, lit, rotating |
| Shaders | A full-screen gradient they can edit live |
| Capstone | A repo, a board, a 4-week milestone on paper |

The point is competence, not coverage.

## Syllabus: what must be written

Put this in a document they can find at 11 pm. Also put a 1-page version in the repo README.

**Required blocks:**

1. Course goal and weekly rhythm (lecture / live coding / lab / homework / quiz)
2. Prerequisites you will actually enforce
3. Software and hardware (and the lab alternative if their laptop cannot)
4. Grade table with weights
5. Late policy (be specific: hours, caps, what “emergency” means)
6. Collaboration policy: what they may share, what they may not
7. AI policy: allowed uses, required disclosure, forbidden uses — see [[Teaching/12 Academic Integrity and AI]]
8. Accessibility and how to request accommodation — see [[Teaching/10 Inclusive Teaching and Accessibility]]
9. Communication: where you will answer (LMS / email / office hours), and your response-time promise
10. Schedule with topics, not just week numbers
11. How to get help before they fail a week
12. What “done” means for the final project

**Tone:** firm and human. “Late work loses 10% per 24 hours, max 50%, except documented emergencies” is better than “no late work” if you will make exceptions anyway. Do not write a rule you will not keep.

## The verbal contract (say this)

> You will be confused in this course. That is normal. The unacceptable thing is staying confused alone for two weeks. Come to lab, office hours, or the forum with a screenshot and what you already tried.

> I will not surprise you with hidden grading criteria. I will surprise you with degenerate inputs.

> I will not pretend AI does not exist. I will require you to be able to explain and modify anything you submit.

## Names

Learn names faster than you think you need to.

- Photo roster if the university provides one
- Name tents for two weeks in smaller rooms
- Call the roster when you return work
- In lab, use names when you sit down: “Maya, show me the test that fails”

Students work harder for a person who knows they exist.

## Diagnostic, not a gate

Day one or week one homework: a short, ungraded or low-stakes diagnostic.

- Can they write a loop and a function?
- Can they clone and run?
- Can they add two vectors?

Use it to assign support, not to shame. If half the room fails vectors, your Week 2 graphics lecture must change. See [[Teaching/02 How Students Learn]].

## Safety and power

On day one, state:

- How to report harassment
- That you will not meet a student alone in a closed room after hours without a public note (use a booked office, door open, or a TA present)
- That grades are not negotiated by charm

You are not their friend. You can be warm and still be their examiner.

## Common first-day failures

- Death by syllabus
- Install session that eats the tiny win
- Promising a Discord you will not moderate
- Joking that “half of you will drop” (some will believe you)
- Over-selling how easy shaders are
- Under-selling the weekly hours

## First-week email (send after day one)

Five lines:

1. Link to syllabus and repo
2. The one task due before meeting 2
3. Office hours
4. “If the starter does not run, come to X with your laptop”
5. The AI/collaboration sentence

## Exercise

Write the 1-page syllabus. Time yourself. If you cannot do it in 40 minutes, the course policies are not decided.
