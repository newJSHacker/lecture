# 23 — First year as an instructor

You do not need to become a legendary teacher by December. You need to run a fair course, learn names, keep the starter running, and change three things in year 2.

This note is a calendar, not a pep talk.

## Thirty days before term

- Read [[Teaching/00 How to Use These Notes]] and the 90-minute path
- Write the 1-page syllabus ([[Teaching/04 First Day and Syllabus]])
- Freeze the student stack (language, repo, one graphics API)
- Build week 1–3 starters until a friend can clone them
- Write the integrity four-liner ([[Teaching/12 Academic Integrity and AI]])
- Find the room, the projector, the lab image, the disability office process
- Ask who your TAs are; if none, cut assessment load now ([[Teaching/15 Time Management for Instructors]])
- Schedule office hours into your calendar as if they were a class
- Ask one experienced instructor: “What surprised you in week 3?”

Do not spend the month redesigning the entire six-semester program. That is [[01 subjects]] work for a later year.

## Week 0 (the week before students)

- Print checklists A, J, K from [[Teaching/19 Checklists and Templates]]
- Record a 4-minute backup of the first demo
- Send the five-line email from [[Teaching/04 First Day and Syllabus]]
- Sleep.

## Weeks 1–3

Goals: names, a tiny win, a diagnostic, a working help system.

- Do not add topics because you are nervous
- Circulate in every lab
- Log four lines after each week
- Return the first graded thing fast, even if it is a tiny quiz
- Invite the quiet students who failed the diagnostic

If week 1 is an install disaster, that is your emergency, not theirs. Fix the starter; do not blame the cohort.

## Weeks 4–7

The novelty is gone. This is where courses die.

- Keep quizzes short
- Stop-the-room when three desks share a bug
- Calibrate TAs before a big grade
- Watch your email hours
- Plan the project spec **now**, not in week 10

Midterm: pictures and traces, not trivia. [[Teaching/09 Assessment Design]]

## Weeks 8–11

Project season. Your job shifts from new content to **diagnosis and cuts**.

- Feature freeze date in writing
- Individual checkpoints so groups cannot hide
- One studio critique with criteria
- Do not start a new framework because you are bored

## Weeks 12–15

- Exhibition and defense logistics ([[Graduation Requirements]], [[Teaching/22 Supervising Theses]])
- Regrade window
- Student evaluations: ask for specific comments on starter, feedback speed, respect
- Write the year-1 vs year-2 note while you still remember

Do not assign a new epic in week 13.

## What “good enough” looks like in year 1

- Students can do the must-layer labs
- Grades are explainable from a rubric
- No safety or harassment case was ignored
- You know who was drowning and you contacted them
- The repo clones
- You have a list of five changes for next year

You will not love every lecture. That is normal.

## What to refuse in year 1

- A third new prep
- Twelve thesis students
- A complete rewrite of the stack in week 5
- Unpaid weekend Discord
- Committees that are not required

Your chair would rather you teach one course well.

## Find people

- A teaching mentor (not only a research advisor)
- A peer to observe ([[Teaching/16 Teaching Portfolio and Growth]])
- A TA you can trust to tell you the starter is bad

Do not isolate. Isolation turns ordinary problems into shame.

## If you came from industry

Your advantage: you have seen production pain; you can live-code for real.

Your gaps: assessment design, accommodations, academic integrity process, the pace of novices.

Read [[Teaching/08 Feedback and Rubrics]], [[Teaching/10 Inclusive Teaching and Accessibility]], [[Teaching/12 Academic Integrity and AI]] twice.

Do not treat students like junior employees who should already know Git. Teaching Git is part of the job.

## If you came from a PhD

Your advantage: you can explain invariants.

Your gap: you may lecture like a seminar and never ship a starter.

Force yourself to do [[Teaching/06 Live Coding Pedagogy]] every week. Watch the clock. Cut the paper.

## End-of-year packet (2 hours)

1. Five changes
2. Two assignments you will keep
3. Anonymized examples of strong / weak work
4. Your four-line logs, skimmed
5. A thank-you to TAs with specific praise

That packet is the beginning of a portfolio.
