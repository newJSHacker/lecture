# 10 — Inclusive teaching and accessibility

Inclusion is not a poster. It is whether a student with a different body, language, money, or background can do the same course without heroic effort.

Graphics courses accidentally exclude people: tiny code, flashing shaders, assumed gaming culture, expensive laptops, “jokes” about who is bad at math.

## Universal design (do these for everyone)

- High-contrast slides and IDE
- Large live-code font
- Captions on recordings
- Materials before class when possible (especially readings and starter)
- Multiple ways to participate: speak, write, type in a shared doc
- Assignments that do not require hearing color names alone (“the red cube”) — also say position and name
- A lab machine path for students whose laptops cannot run WebGL well
- Text descriptions of key diagrams in the notes

These help more people than the one student who requested them.

## Legal accommodations

If the disability office sends a letter (extra time, note-taker, rest breaks, flexible attendance), you implement it. You do not diagnose. You do not bargain it down because “industry will not give extra time.” Industry is not your exam hall.

Keep a private checklist. Never announce an accommodation to the class.

## Vision, hearing, motor

| Barrier | Classroom move |
| --- | --- |
| Low vision | 160% editor, verbalize what you point at, provide files |
| Color vision | Never encode meaning in color only; use shape, label, dash |
| Hearing | Mic, captions, do not talk facing the board only |
| Motor / fatigue | Extra time, less live-typing pressure, allow speech-to-text |
| Photosensitivity | Warn before flashing shaders; provide a still alternative |

Seizures and migraine are not theoretical in a shader course. Warn.

## Language

Many IGWT students are not native speakers of the instruction language.

- Slow down technical nouns; write them
- Allow a glossary
- Grade code and diagrams more than idiomatic essays, unless writing is the goal
- Do not mock accents or “broken English”
- Pairing: do not always put the weakest speaker with the strongest coder as unpaid translator

## Money and equipment

Do not require a personal VR headset in week 2. Provide a checkout pool or a shared lab hour.

Do not require paid software when Blender, a browser, and a text editor will do.

Be careful with “buy this course on Udemy” as unofficial homework.

## Culture and belonging

- Use a variety of names and examples (not only sci-fi guns and anime)
- Do not assume everyone played games or knows Twitch
- Interrupt jokes about gender, nationality, or “graphics bros”
- Call on a range of people; do not let one student become the room
- Show work by a range of practitioners in slide credits

Belonging is not a pizza party. It is whether a student can ask a basic question in week 3.

## Neurodiversity

Some students cannot take a surprise oral. Some cannot sit a 3-hour lab without a break. Some hyperfocus and skip the report.

- Publish agendas
- Avoid sudden changes of room or stack
- Allow noise-cancelling headphones in lab if they can still hear a stop-the-room call
- Written instructions, not only spoken

## Religion, fasting, family

Be careful with evening demos in the last week of a fasting month, and with Sunday deadlines if your population cannot use that day. A Monday 09:00 deadline is often kinder than Sunday 23:59.

## Content warnings (rare but real)

Medical visualization, some anatomy assets, and violent game references can be hard. Offer an alternate asset when the learning goal is the pipeline, not the picture.

## What inclusion is not

- Lowering the standard of the kernel
- Doing the project for a student
- Hiding feedback to be “nice”

High standards and high support. That is the pair.
