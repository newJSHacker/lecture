# 16 — Teaching portfolio and growth

You will not become good by intending to. You become good by looking at evidence and changing one practice.

A teaching portfolio is that evidence, arranged so a hiring committee, a promotion file, or your future self can see it.

## What to keep (from week 1)

- Syllabus versions (date them)
- Two sample assignments + rubrics + anonymized student work (with permission or campus policy)
- A weekly log: what failed, what you changed ([[Teaching/01 What Good Teaching Looks Like]])
- Student evaluations **and** your written response (what you accept, what you reject, what you will change)
- A peer observation (someone sits in; you sit in on them)
- One recording of live coding
- Project showcase links (class, not individual grades)

Do not keep only the glowing emails.

## A simple portfolio structure

1. **Teaching statement** (2 pages): what you think learning is, how you teach IGWT, one example that went wrong and what you changed
2. **Course list** with your role
3. **Representative materials** (one course designed end-to-end is stronger than seven slide decks)
4. **Evidence of student learning** (before/after quiz, project run rates, not only smiles)
5. **Improvement loop** (year 1 vs year 2 of the same course)
6. **Service to teaching** (TA training, curriculum, [[01 subjects]] work)

Committees can smell a statement made of slogans. Use a concrete scene: the week the starter broke, the week you added a planted bug, the week orals replaced a take-home.

## Observing and being observed

Ask a colleague to watch 20 minutes with a form:

| Question | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| What was the learning goal? | |
| Who spoke? | |
| When did students do something? | |
| Was the demo visible? | |
| What would you try next time? | |

Then you watch them. Mutual observation is less threatening and more useful than a single “evaluation visit.”

## Student evaluations (how to read them)

They measure a mix of learning, entertainment, gender and accent bias, and whether the projector worked.

**Use:** recurring comments about pace, clarity, feedback speed, respect.

**Do not use as sole truth:** a single cruel paragraph; a score without comments; “more entertaining.”

Write a 200-word response each term: three changes you will make, one thing you will not change and why (for example, you will keep weekly quizzes).

## Scholarship of teaching

You do not need a journal paper. You can:

- A/B a starter (with ethics approval if required)
- Count how many pairs pass the hidden test after you change the lecture
- Present a teaching talk in the department

Treat teaching questions like engineering questions: hypothesis, change, evidence.

## Career paths

- **New instructor:** survive with [[Teaching/23 First Year as an Instructor]]
- **Job market:** one designed course + portfolio site + a talk that is not only research
- **Promotion:** year-over-year improvement and leadership (TAs, curriculum)
- **Industry-to-academia:** your advantage is live practice; your gap is assessment and inclusion — study those notes

## Rest is part of growth

A portfolio built on 70-hour weeks is a warning label. See [[Teaching/15 Time Management for Instructors]].
